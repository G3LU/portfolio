I build LLM agents that turn a plain-language inspection request into the CAD faces a robot should check.

I’m Jaeyoung Lee, an AI engineer working on manufacturing. I started as a smart-factory project manager at a battery cathode-material plant. There, a model tuned for one machine didn’t carry over to the next, and every change meant someone redoing the setup by hand.

I took that problem into research at the Korea Institute of Industrial Technology (KITECH), where I worked on LLM agents grounded in CAD geometry, small domain models that train on a single GPU, and search over encrypted data.

Inspection planning

Robot vision inspection is taught again whenever the part changes. An engineer decides once more what to look at, where the camera goes and how the robot moves. For two years at KITECH I worked on replacing that step with a CAD file and a sentence. It came out as five pieces of work, three conference papers and two journal manuscripts now under review, each picking up what the previous one left open.

A multi-agent planner from a prompt and a CAD file to an inspection path

ICCAS 2025, first author. “A Hierarchical Supervisor Architecture for Coverage Path Planning in Robotic Quality Inspection.”

Coverage path planning normally aims for uniform geometric coverage. But defects are not equally likely, or equally serious, everywhere on a part, so a uniform plan spends much of its time on low-risk surfaces. This first system takes a prompt and a STEP file and returns an ordered robot path that concentrates on the surfaces that matter.

How it works

  • Three supervisors. A global supervisor splits the job and passes one shared pipeline state through a processing supervisor and an inspection supervisor, so each stage’s output is the next stage’s input.
  • Reading the request. A three-stage router classifies the prompt: keyword match first, then k-nearest neighbors over 3,000 labeled example prompts, and an 8B Llama model only when the classifier’s confidence falls between 0.65 and 0.85.
  • Reading the part. A STEP parser built on pythonocc-core walks the B-Rep and records each face’s surface type, area, curvature and topology.
  • Where to measure. Each face gets an importance score, area times curvature. The faces that make up 80% of the total importance receive measurement points in proportion to their score, placed in each surface’s own parameter space so that they lie exactly on the geometry.
  • Where to stand, and in what order. Candidate viewpoints are filtered by field of view and incidence angle, the smallest covering set is chosen greedily, and a nearest-neighbor tour orders them.

Result

Tested in simulation on an automotive clutch component with the optics of an Intel RealSense D435i, against a uniform grid of viewpoints.

Half the stops and a 41% shorter path, with every measurement point on the critical surfaces still covered.

KITECH filed this planner as a Korean patent application in August 2025 (KR 10-2025-0109979), with me as a co-inventor.

What it left open

The request was only sorted into a category, surface, edge or hole. It was not tied to specific faces. Importance came from area and curvature alone, so edges, fillets and hole walls went unaddressed, and robot kinematics were not checked.

Grounding the prompt in exact B-Rep geometry

IEEE SMC 2026, first author. “From Intent to Visual Inspection: LLM-Driven Inspection Planning Grounded in CAD Boundary Representation.” With co-authors at KITECH, Sungkyunkwan University and Georgia Tech.

Instructions such as “inspect the hole next to the largest surface” or “focus on curved boundaries” name geometry by description. A CAD model’s boundary representation already holds what is needed to resolve them: exact surfaces, and a record of which faces, edges and vertices touch. This system lets a language model reason over that structure without ever producing geometry itself.

How it works

  1. Perception
    The prompt is parsed for sensor settings and intent. The part becomes a graph of faces, edges and vertices, and every face gets normalized scores for area, hole count, curvature and edge sharpness, weighted per prompt.
  2. Reasoning
    The model does not output face IDs. In up to five iterations it picks one of three predefined operators and its arguments: filter by attribute, expand to adjacent entities, or rank by task-weighted importance. A bad call returns a structured error it can correct. If no valid region results, a deterministic rule-based fallback applies and the case is logged as a failure.
  3. Action
    The region is clustered into inspection units, sampled with measurement points suited to surfaces, edges and vertices, and covered by viewpoints chosen with greedy set cover after ray-casting visibility checks against the exact faces.

Results

Three parts from the ABC dataset with 36, 69 and 90 faces, a RealSense D435 sensor model, and Qwen2.5-Coder 32B served locally with no cloud API.

  • Grounding. Nine prompt and part combinations, from attribute-level to multi-step relational prompts, scored against reference regions computed without any LLM. Face-level F1 ranged from 0.41 to 1.00, and six of the nine matched exactly.
  • Compact plans. 4 to 10 viewpoints per task, with 82.6% to 96.8% of the measurement points covered. In one case 226 selected features reduced to 28 inspection units covered from six viewpoints.
  • Repeatability. Five LLM backbones, five runs per condition: the selected faces overlapped by 95.6% or more (pairwise Jaccard). One backbone scored 100% only because its structured output always failed and the fallback took over.
  • Time. 71 seconds per plan on average, about two thirds of it geometry processing.

Under a whole-part instruction, compared with three geometry-driven ways of proposing viewpoints, all using the same visibility checks and the same greedy selection:

It had the highest coverage per viewpoint on all three parts. On part B, 10 viewpoints reached about the coverage that vertex sampling needed 59 for. Dense space sampling still covers more in absolute terms, so this is a planner for task-focused inspection, not a replacement for exhaustive coverage.

What it left open

Reference regions existed for nine cases on three parts, which is not a benchmark. And as the failure above shows, some requests cannot be resolved without asking.

Checking that an inferred region is the right one

IEEE CASE 2026, first author. “Benchmarking Prompt-Conditioned ROI Inference via GT-Anchored Validation on Boundary Representation CAD Models.”

An inferred region goes straight into planning. Select too little and critical surfaces are missed. Select too much and viewpoints and cycle time grow. Until this point inferred regions were treated as an intermediate output and never checked against a defined target.

How it works

A validation protocol on MFInstSeg, a public dataset of machined parts with face-level feature annotations. Each prompt is turned into a structured query. A target region is then built from the annotations by rules specific to six prompt families: a named feature, the bottom of a feature, the surface carrying a feature, a surface ranked by size, a spatial relation between features, and the whole model. The inferred region is scored in three ways: whether the right target was selected, how well the final region overlaps it by area, and where it differs in position, size and boundary.

From 9,375 test models I generated 343,624 candidate prompt cases, removed the 22,679 whose target could not be derived without ambiguity, and kept 320,945. Of those, 3,000 were run end to end, 500 per family.

Results

The ranked-surface row is the finding. The target was selected correctly every time and the final region was still wrong, off in position, size and boundary as well as in overlap. The cause was not a misread prompt: the selected surface was expanded into faces more conservatively than the target region is defined. Selecting the right feature and producing the right region are different problems, so a region needs geometric validation before it is wired into a planner. A second 8B model, Llama 3.1, showed the same pattern, with 0.981 and 0.852 overall.

What it left open

This measures regions. It does not make them more reliable. The next step holds the model’s answer to evidence it has actually retrieved.

A CAD agent that has to show its evidence

Journal manuscript under review. The main result of my M.S. research.

A recognized feature does not settle what to inspect. The same hole calls for different regions under general inspection, wall inspection and rim-burr inspection. “Check the hole rims for burrs” may concern the hole wall, the opening rim, the surface the hole sits in and an adjacent chamfer or fillet, and these serve different purposes in a plan.

The agent takes a STEP model and a request and returns the faces to inspect, each with a role: Target for the subject of the request, Context for what is needed to locate and interpret it, and Envelope for the rims, chamfers and transitions where the defect may appear. The language model interprets the request and chooses which tool to call next. It never computes geometry, measurement points or camera poses.

How a request becomes a plan

  1. Read the CAD modelDeterministic code
    OpenCascade turns the STEP file into B-Rep evidence: faces, edges and their adjacency, hole-like structures, opening and rim candidates, host and transition cues, and connected face patches.
  2. Gather evidence and proposeLanguage model and tools
    Over several turns the model calls typed CAD-analysis tools, under a nominal budget of 12 calls, and proposes groups of faces with roles.
  3. Complete the proposalDeterministic code
    A controller completes feature instances and attaches adjacent rings, using only faces the tools returned, and records where every face and role assignment came from.
  4. VerifyDeterministic code
    Identifier, provenance and schema checks. A face ID that never appeared in a tool result is removed, with at most two constrained regenerations. If no validated Target remains, the complete rule-based result is substituted.
  5. PlanDeterministic code
    A fixed planner turns the region into measurement points and viewpoints, filtering by working distance, field of view, incidence and occlusion against the exact B-Rep.

Design choices

  • A local model. Qwen3-Coder 30B through Ollama at temperature 0 with a fixed seed and no external API. The exact model binary is pinned by a digest and checked on every case.
  • Sixteen typed tools in four groups. Global and feature analysis: CAD summary, feature classifier, importance scoring, property ranking, feature details. Candidate retrieval: feature filtering, spatial filtering, family ranking, description match. Patch, boundary and neighborhood analysis: patch expansion, patch summary, patch boundary, patch neighbors, adjacency search. Instance organization: isolation detection, spatial clustering. No tool returns a region, a viewpoint or a coverage value.
  • Ten expert rules as the fallback. Four assign Target, three Context and three Envelope. The rule result replaces a failed answer outright. The two are never merged.
  • Traceable output. Each face and role assignment stores its origin and the tool evidence behind it.

Results

300 request and model cases built from 42 parts of MFInstSeg: 200 on smaller parts (22 parts, median 29.5 faces) and 100 on larger ones (20 parts, median 72), balanced over four kinds of request: a whole feature family, selected instances, relations between features, and numeric conditions. Scores are against a reference built by one reproducible rule: annotated feature faces as Target, the adjacent ring of faces as Context, and the next ring as Envelope.

Region recovery improved over every alternative. Against the rule-based method the F1 gain is 0.143 [0.044, 0.257] on smaller parts and 0.134 [0.051, 0.228] on larger ones, with 95% bootstrap intervals over parts. Most of it comes from the Envelope: recall of those faces is 0.844 against 0.586, and 0.792 against 0.572.

  • Fallback, not blending. The rule substitution fired in 7 of 300 cases and added 0.011 and 0.030 F1. Merging the agent’s faces with the rules’ faces instead raises F1 to 0.781 and 0.724 but drops κ to 0.122 and 0.132, below the rules alone.
  • Roles and planning effort. In an ablation on reference regions, planning with all three roles instead of the Target alone raised mean per-face coverage from 0.585 to 0.680 on smaller parts and from 0.435 to 0.631 on larger ones. The price was measurement points, 66.6 to 109.2 and 117.1 to 240.8 on average, while the number of viewpoints barely changed.
  • The planner on its own. Supplementary studies compare its viewpoint generation with sampling baselines, test the observability of synthetic defects, and transfer selected viewpoints to a simulated UR5e robot cell, where they kept most of their coverage.

What the numbers do and don’t show

The reference regions cover 67% and 61% of a part’s faces on average, so selecting every face scores a higher F1 than any method, at a κ of zero. That is why κ is reported next to F1, with select-all and random selection as reference points. Read that way, four things remain open, and the manuscript says so.

  • The κ gain over the rule-based method, 0.027 and 0.041, has intervals that include zero. The agent also selects more faces than the rules do: 80.1% and 71.2% of a part, against 60.0% and 57.4%.
  • Of 10,555 accepted face and role assignments, the model itself cited 34%. Deterministic controller steps produced the rest, so the gain belongs to the model and controller together, not to the language model alone.
  • Role labels match the reference’s role partition for only 28% to 43% of labeled faces. Recovering the region works better than dividing it into roles.
  • Predicted regions were not yet run through the planner under a controlled protocol, so no planning-cost saving over whole-surface inspection is claimed.

The benchmark, 800 retained controller traces and the analysis scripts are archived on Zenodo, and every table can be regenerated from them with one command.

The one question worth asking before inspecting

Journal manuscript under review.

“Inspect the rectangular through step,” on a part with two such steps, can mean the larger one (1,607 mm²), the smaller one (512 mm²), or both. Before committing to faces, an assistant can ask one yes-or-no question. The usual criterion, expected information gain, cannot choose here: with no prior preference, each of the three single-reading questions carries the same 0.918 bits. Their consequences are not the same.

Asking about the smaller step wins because a “no” leaves two readings that share the larger step, and inspecting both steps then covers either one at little extra cost. If the requester meant both, a tie-break that asks about the first candidate listed ends at a realized cost of 0.85, with most of the intended area missed. The cost-based question ends at 0.15.

So I score each question by the expected reduction in inspection cost after its answer. This is the classical one-step value of information, instantiated on B-Rep face sets. The contribution is not a new criterion. It is the geometric form, an account of when it matters, and a measurement on a public dataset.

Results

  • When it matters. For disjoint regions of equal weight, equal-size questions tie. On a nested chain of three regions, the question that isolates the middle one is never optimal. Any overlap between two of three equal regions breaks the tie, linearly in the overlap. All three are proved in the manuscript.
  • How often. On 2,550 MFInstSeg parts and 12,125 ambiguous cases with three or more readings, the cost spread among equally informative questions is 1.3 to 3.3 times larger for nested readings than for disjoint ones.
  • How much. On 741 held-out cases with exact STEP surface areas, committing without asking costs 0.569 on average. One question chosen by information gain brings that to 0.236, and one chosen by inspection cost to 0.189. On nested readings the difference is 0.084 [0.077, 0.091].
  • Against a rule of thumb. “Isolate the reading that overlaps least with the others” comes within 0.003 on nested readings, but no single rule holds up across all conditions.
  • Against a language model. A 35B model shown the candidate list picked the cost-optimal question in 31% of cases, about as well as information gain with a random tie-break. Asked to write code that computes the answer, only 24% of its outputs ran.
  • More than one question. With a price per question, a greedy stopping rule on the same score stays within 0.0007 of the exact optimal policy.
  • Scope. A computational benchmark with a simulated requester. It does not measure industrial savings or how people respond to the questions.

A 3B model that reasons about additive manufacturing, trained on one GPU

IICC 2025, first author. “Enhancing Domain-Specific Reasoning in sLLM: Fine-Tuning with RAG and LoRA.” The paper is in Korean.

Retrieval-augmented generation answers simple domain questions well, such as how laser powder bed fusion differs from binder jetting. It does worse when a question needs several steps of reasoning. I built a workflow that turns one domain document into reasoning-style training data and teaches a 3B model to think before it answers.

  1. Generate questions
    Loaded “A Guide to Additive Manufacturing,” split it into chunks, embedded them with nomic-embed-text-v1 into a FAISS index, and had Llama 3.3 70B write 10,000 questions across six topics: concepts, standardization, design principles, process workflow, application cases and material development.
  2. Add the reasoning
    DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen 32B wrote a reasoning trace and an answer for every question. The 70B variant needed about six hours per batch of 1,000, so I moved to the 32B.
  3. Fine-tune
    Llama 3.2 3B with <think> tokens added, which meant retraining the token embeddings and the output head, plus LoRA on the attention and feed-forward modules: 812 million trainable parameters, about 27% of the model. Five epochs over 9,000 examples took 191 minutes on one A100 40GB. Training and validation loss fell together, from 1.94 to 1.13.
  4. Evaluate
    1,500 questions sampled from the set, with Qwen2.5 72B as judge scoring accuracy, logic and conciseness out of 100.

The fine-tuned model scored highest on all three criteria: 3.725 points above the four comparison models on average, and 1.77 above the Llama 3.2 base it started from. The dataset is public on Hugging Face as g3lu/additive_manufacturing_reasoning, and the LoRA adapter is published there too.

An LLM judge has known biases, toward verbose answers and toward models like itself, so these scores are a first reading. The paper names judges of other architectures as the next step.

Finding similar defect cases without exposing the data

KITECH, in collaboration with CryptoLab.

When a new defect shows up, the first move is to find similar past cases. But defect images and their descriptions reveal product geometry and process capability, so they are hard to put on a server someone else runs. Ordinary vector search doesn’t solve this, because the server still holds the embeddings in plaintext.

I developed a retrieval pipeline that computes similarity on encrypted vectors. Defect images and text descriptions are embedded with CLIP and aligned with a linear projection, so a photo and a written symptom both work as a query. Cause metadata generated with Llama 3.1 8B is added to the index.

Client
  • Embeds the query and encrypts it
  • Decrypts the scores and picks the top results
Encrypted query to the server
Encrypted scores back to the client
Server
  • Stores embeddings as CKKS ciphertexts
  • Computes inner products on ciphertexts
  • Never sees plaintext vectors or the results

CKKS arithmetic is approximate, so the question was whether quality and speed survive encryption. Compared with plaintext search, scores deviated by at most 0.0006 and a query took about 30 ms.

Patent application: planning an inspection path from a CAD model and a prompt

Korean patent application 10-2025-0109979, filed 8 August 2025. Applicant: KITECH. Co-inventor. “Method and System for Optimizing Inspection Path Planning of 3D CAD Objects with Agentic Artificial Intelligence.”

Inspection path planning today mostly relies on fixed path-generation algorithms. They work in the setting they were built for, but they do not adapt when the part has an irregular shape or when the purpose of the inspection changes. This application covers the planner described in the ICCAS 2025 paper: a multi-agent system that takes a 3D CAD model and a written inspection requirement and returns an optimized inspection path. It has 15 claims, and examination has been requested.

What the method does

  1. Take the request
    3D CAD data and a natural-language prompt that states what has to be inspected.
  2. Decide what kind of target it is
    The prompt is first compared, by embedding similarity, with stored example sentences for each type of target. If the best match falls below a threshold, an LLM interprets the prompt instead. Clear requests stay fast, and vague ones still get read properly.
  3. Read only the geometry that matters
    For a surface, an edge or a hole, only the matching elements of the B-Rep are traversed, and curvature, normals and bounding boxes are extracted.
  4. Place measurement points
    On a surface, more points where the curvature is high, with farthest-point sampling to keep them apart. In a hole, the axis, radius and depth are computed and points are placed on a grid around the axis and along the depth.
  5. Choose viewpoints
    Candidate viewpoints are checked for visibility of every measurement point. A greedy set cover gives a first covering set, and integer linear programming then reduces it to the smallest one.
  6. Order the path
    A traveling-salesman solver finds the shortest tour through the chosen viewpoints.

Also claimed

  • The system as three agents. A supervisor that controls the flow, a processing agent with the prompt router and STEP parser, and an inspection agent with the measurement-point generator, viewpoint generator and path planner.
  • A 3D view. Measurement points, viewpoints and the path are drawn over the CAD model so that the plan can be checked by eye.
  • Plans kept as knowledge. Each plan is embedded and stored in a vector database, so later questions about similar inspections can be answered from past plans with retrieval-augmented generation.

Patent application: viewpoints that hold up when the lighting changes

Korean patent application 10-2025-0189255, filed 3 December 2025. Applicant: KITECH. Co-inventor. “Apparatus for Setting Viewpoint Based on Digital Twin for Vision Inspection and Method Thereof.”

Viewpoints sampled from a CAD model assume ideal, unchanging conditions. On a real line the light changes with aging lamps, differences between lines, an open window, the weather, the hour and the season, and detection models are sensitive to all of it. A viewpoint that looked right in CAD then has to be adjusted on site, and in high-mix production that means teaching the robot again for each product.

This application covers a way to test viewpoints against changing light before the line exists, in a digital twin. It has 14 claims, and examination has been requested.

How it works

  1. Mark what to inspect
    The part’s 3D CAD file is loaded, and the regions that need inspection are defined on it as inspection points.
  2. Add virtual defects
    Defects that occur in real processes are generated on the CAD model: blowholes, burrs and cracks.
  3. Sample candidate viewpoints
    By space sampling, vertex sampling or patch sampling, so that every inspection point can be imaged from somewhere.
  4. Vary the light in a digital twin
    For each candidate the lighting is randomized in intensity, color temperature, position and angle, and an object detector from the R-CNN, YOLO or SSD families looks for the virtual defects, over and over. The described embodiment runs this in Isaac Sim.
  5. Score and choose
    Each viewpoint gets two scores: how much the detector’s confidence varies across lighting conditions, and the share of conditions in which that confidence stays at 0.8 or above. For each region, the viewpoint that stays high and steady is selected.

The result is a set of viewpoints chosen for robustness before a product reaches the line, which cuts down the repeated adjustment and robot teaching.
